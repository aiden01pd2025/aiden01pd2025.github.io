Final project: Exploring spline curves

How does a computer turn a handful of points into a smooth curve? This slideshow answers that by building splines from the ground up, with sliders and animations that let you drag the control points and watch the curve respond.

The approach is to start with the simplest possible piece, a straight line that blends between two points, and ask what goes wrong when you chain pieces together. The answer is corners, so the project defines levels of smoothness (\(C^0\), \(C^1\), \(C^2\)) and then looks for cubic curves that meet them. Each spline type comes from choosing a different set of conditions and solving for a matrix: Hermite splines pin down endpoints and directions, Bézier curves repeatedly blend points together (De Casteljau's algorithm), and B-splines demand smoothness across every joint at the cost of no longer passing through the control points. Curvature combs make the differences visible.

The deck closes with where each kind is used, from physics simulations to vector graphics to cameras, and a look ahead at NURBS and surface splines.

Built with Reveal.js, Manim, and Plotly.js.

Project 1: Vectors

Two problems, one toolkit: the dot and cross product. Both come with movable 3D graphs and animations.

Skew lines. Lines in space that never meet and are never parallel are called skew. The project first proves that a pair of lines is skew, using the cross product to rule out parallel lines and row reduction to rule out a shared point. It then sandwiches the two lines between parallel planes, uses the cross product as the planes' normal direction, and projects a vector between the planes onto that normal to get the gap. Repeating the steps with variables instead of numbers gives a formula for any pair of skew lines.

Genetics. Allele frequencies describe each population, and the project turns them into vectors so populations can be compared by angle. Taking square roots first makes every vector a unit vector, which means the dot product is exactly the cosine similarity, and it connects to a standard statistical measure of how close two distributions are. From there it mixes populations as weighted combinations of vectors and uses calculus to find the blend that sits closest to a target population.

Built with R Markdown, Plotly (R), Manim, and ggplot2.

Project 2: Race car cornering

What does it take to keep a race car on the track? This project answers with vector-valued functions, animating the position, velocity, and acceleration vectors of a car as it takes a corner.

Flat turns. Each turn at the Walt Disney World Speedway is modeled as a circular arc, and differentiating the position gives velocity and acceleration. Ranking the turns by curvature alone gives one answer, but ranking them by the normal component of acceleration, which accounts for speed, reorders them. The tightest turn is not necessarily the hardest.

Banked turns. At Bristol Motor Speedway, the project starts from a force diagram of gravity, the normal force, and friction on a banked curve. Balancing the vertical forces gives the normal force, summing the horizontal ones gives the centripetal force, and setting that equal to what the circular motion requires gives the fastest speed a car can take the turn. Mass drops out of the result. The model is then used to compare dry and wet pavement, and run backwards to estimate how grippy a car's tires are from its speed.

Built with R Markdown and animated vector diagrams.

Project 3: Big ball tumors

Hyperthermia therapy cooks a tumor with heat, and doctors need to know how much of it has reached an effective temperature. This project sets up that question as a volume problem, then solves it with multiple integration. The 3D graphs are movable, and animations show the nested surfaces of equal temperature (equitherms) inside a tumor.

It starts with a perfect sphere, where the key insight is that volume grows with the cube of the radius, so a core that looks like a big share of the radius is a much smaller share of the volume. Real tumors are wrinkled, so the project models them as bumpy spheres whose radius changes with direction, written in spherical coordinates. Their exact volume comes from triple integrals, with symmetry and Wallis' formula taming the trigonometric powers, and each answer is checked against a graphing calculator.

Built with R Markdown and Plotly (R).

Final project: Clash Royale cards and decks

What makes a good Clash Royale deck? This project pulls card data from the official API and usage and win rates from RoyaleAPI, then looks for patterns that separate strong cards and decks from weak ones.

After cleaning the data (recoding missing values, filling gaps using game knowledge, and encoding categories as numbers), the team asked two questions. First, which card traits predict a high win rate? A logistic regression, trained to separate cards above and below the median win rate, found only modest predictive power, with one standout: cards that can target both air and ground tend to win less. Second, what roles do cards play? K-prototypes clustering, which handles a mix of numeric and categorical features, sorted cards into roles like damage dealers, tanks, cycle cards, buildings, and spells. Mapping top decks onto those roles, and checking which roles appear together, revealed common deck archetypes. The slideshow embeds interactive charts, including a 3D view of the clusters.

A team project for DATA 11900, built with Python, scikit-learn, K-prototypes, and Jupyter RISE.
